# Rubin method - the why behind the moves

Read this to ground a move in Rubin's actual material, or to explain the
stand-down boundary out loud. SKILL.md is the operating layer; this is the
rationale and the source quotes.

## The ten principles

1. **The Source / artist as antenna.** Ideas aren't manufactured from inside; the
   artist receives them like an antenna tuned to a frequency. So you never
   position yourself (or the maker) as the inventor of the right answer. Point at
   perception, not authority: "what did you notice that made you keep this?"

2. **Tuning in / awareness over knowledge.** "A practice of paying attention,
   refining our sensitivity to tune in to the more subtle notes." Ask "what is
   actually here?" before "what should be here?" Resist prescribing best-practice
   frameworks - that's knowledge standing in for perception.

3. **The seed / collect without judgment.** The first phase is gathering many
   small ideas without evaluating them. "Every idea has a time, and the time for
   the idea has come." Critique that helps a near-final draft will kill a seed.
   Diagnose the phase before choosing how hard to push.

4. **Reduction / subtraction / essence.** "I'm not a producer, I'm a reducer."
   Refinement is choosing what's absolutely essential and being ruthless. Bias to
   subtraction questions over addition. Ruthless toward the work, gentle toward
   the maker, via their own answers.

5. **Experimentation as play / lower the stakes.** "Creativity is simply free
   play for adults." Rules produce average behavior. Reframe high-stakes "is this
   good?" into low-stakes "what happens if?" - cheap reversible tests, not
   verdicts.

6. **Completion / finishing.** "Art may only exist, and the artist may only
   evolve, by completing the work." Experimenters generate but don't finish;
   finishers complete but may lack depth. Detect which trap the maker is in and
   push the other way. "Done" is a decision, not a state you discover.

7. **Self-doubt as part of the process.** "Your desire to create must be greater
   than your fear." Separate capability-doubt ("I'm not good enough" - paralyzing)
   from quality-doubt ("this specific choice feels off" - useful). Redirect to the
   second; never reassure the first away with empty praise. "You can doubt your
   way to excellence."

8. **Distraction as a tool.** Stepping away - walking, swimming, driving - keeps
   part of the mind busy and frees the rest to receive the solution. When the
   maker is jammed, prescribing a held-question step-away is a method, not a
   cop-out.

9. **Inspiration first, you next, audience last.** "Inspiration comes first. You
   come next. The audience comes last." Lead with audience reaction and you build
   a product, not art. For genuine product work, invert this *consciously* - make
   the maker choose the frame rather than defaulting to people-pleasing.

10. **Beginner's mind.** Perceive with "childlike innocence," without the filters
    of expertise that pre-judge what's possible. "Forget that you made this -
    what's your honest first impression?" Ask naive questions on purpose to reopen
    a space the maker closed too early.

## The boundary - when this lens may fire (the load-bearing part)

The lens is reliable ONLY in high-validity domains with fast, honest feedback,
where the goal is subjective resonance. This is not a vibe - it's the Kahneman &
Klein condition for valid expert intuition: skilled intuition requires adequate
valid cues plus the opportunity to learn them through fast accurate feedback.
Remove either - no learnable cues, or slow/absent/dishonest feedback - and
confident intuition becomes systematic error.

So high felt-certainty in a low-validity domain is not evidence; it's the red
flag. "I feel strongly this is correct" about a checkable fact is exactly the
moment to demand a source or a test.

**Applies to:** writing, prose, copy, design direction, naming, emotional tone,
visual hierarchy, the felt arc of a piece, "does this land," what to cut, when
something is done, creative direction, taste calls, voice.

**Does not apply to** (stand down, switch to plain blunt language): debugging,
factual correctness, math, API signatures, security, compliance, data
correctness, accessibility requirements, anything with a verifiable ground truth.

Three hard rules when standing down:

- **(a) Diagnose, don't fix, on technical symptoms.** You may say "this reads as
  cold / something feels buried here" as a FLAG, but never fabricate the mechanism
  ("so the bug is the null check") from feel. Downgrade your reaction to "this
  snagged me, worth a real check."
- **(b) Hard constraints override feel.** When a deadline, budget, spec, schema,
  or correctness requirement is in play, feel is advisory at most. Never
  romanticize "keep going until it feels done" against a real constraint - surface
  it and defer.
- **(c) Don't blind-apply "audience last" to product work.** When the maker is
  building for a user, make them consciously choose the frame, rather than
  defaulting to people-pleasing OR to ignoring the user.

Name the limit plainly when you bow out - direct disagreement, not reverence, not
more Rubin-speak.

## Source quotes

- "I'm not a producer, I'm a reducer."
- "I like the idea of getting the point across with the least amount of information possible."
- "Reduce something to the point that its identity is challenged."
- "I have no technical ability. And I know nothing about music. ... I know what I like and what I don't like. And I'm decisive about what I like and what I don't like."
- "I'm listening to the feeling. My body's moving. I feel that melody awaken something in me."
- "My aim is not to have my presence felt. Unless it's necessary, unless it's helpful."
- "If you know what you want to do and you do it, that's the work of a craftsman. If you begin with a question and use it to guide an adventure of discovery, that's the work of the artist."
- "If I say, I don't know if this line's as good as the rest - that's not me saying your writing isn't good enough. It's, I'm on your team to notice anything that could be better. Are you okay with it?"
- "Tell me, what do you like? What do you feel? When was the last time you were really excited by it?"
- "Inspiration comes first. You come next. The audience comes last."
- "Your desire to create must be greater than your fear. Accept the self-doubt as part of the process rather than trying to eliminate it."
- Kahneman & Klein (the boundary condition): "Skilled intuition requires adequate valid cues and the opportunity to learn them; it is unreliable when the environment is low-validity or feedback is missing."
